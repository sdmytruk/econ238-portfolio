Storm Damages, Heat Risk & Adaptation

Weather hazards do not translate directly into deaths or economic losses. Where people live, how vulnerable they are, and the resources available to adapt can change the outcome.

Parts A–C

Severe weather does not show one simple trend.

Across the 84-year period, the assignment counts 20,387 combined events. Looking at the individual series, the peaks and trendlines do not show one obvious pattern.

Floods are higher in some recent periods than in the 1940s, while tornado counts are lower than in the earliest decades. The raw event counts alone cannot tell us how much protection or adaptation has changed.

Part D

Population changes the interpretation.

1.88 → 0.61

combined tornado, flood, and hurricane deaths per million people per year, comparing the 1940s with the 2010s.

Raw deaths fluctuate, but “death intensity” shows a much clearer long-run decline.

This suggests Americans became substantially less likely to die from these three storm hazards on a per-person basis. The 2000s interrupt the decline, but recent death intensity remains well below the earliest decades.

Death intensity = average annual weather deaths ÷ average population × 1,000,000.

The coastline population increase complicates a simple story of people avoiding environmental risk.

Population growth is not the same as migration—births and international immigration matter too—but the data still show more people living in hurricane-exposed coastal counties. Falling storm mortality per person therefore cannot be explained only by people moving away from hazards.

Relocation thought experiment: under the assignment's assumptions, gradually moving vulnerable capital to higher ground costs about $2.4 billion per year for 50 years, approximately 0.008% of 2025 U.S. GDP. The calculation leaves out land prices, infrastructure, community disruption, and structures that cannot realistically be moved.
Part G • IPCC SREX

Why can disaster losses rise even without a proven rise in hazard severity?

Hazard

The physical weather event itself.

Exposure

More people and economic assets located where the event can reach them.

Vulnerability

Differences in wealth, infrastructure, institutions, preparation, and recovery capacity.

The SREX material in the assignment emphasizes exposure and vulnerability as major drivers of long-term economic disaster losses.

Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability. Environmental economics therefore asks not only whether the climate hazard changes, but also how incentives and investment change exposure and vulnerability.
Part H

Heat complicates the storm-mortality story.

1,021

heat deaths recorded in 1995, compared with 30 tornado deaths, 80 flood deaths, and 17 hurricane deaths.

The assignment identifies age, chronic illness, impaired mobility, and inability to care for oneself as important risk factors during major heat waves.

Adaptation matters: warning systems, cooling centers, air conditioning, emergency planning, and checking on vulnerable people can change mortality even when temperatures themselves cannot be avoided.
Conversation • Heat stress

One temperature increase does not affect everyone equally.

My first reaction was that climate change could contribute to heat-related deaths both directly and indirectly.

Direct heat exposure matters, but the conversation also made me think about food, healthcare, stress, and other effects of a changing climate. The mechanism does not look the same for everyone.

Where someone lives matters, but so do their living conditions. Someone who can spend most of the day in an air-conditioned apartment, briefly walk outside, afford nutritious food, and avoid other major stressors experiences the same hot day very differently from someone who is unhoused or cannot afford to escape the heat.

“Heat-stress isn't dealt with equally everywhere.”

The margin of heat-related death therefore depends on more than latitude or temperature. Income, housing, access to cooling, health, food costs, and other stressors can change how close someone already is to that margin.

The economics: if vulnerability is unequal, the value of adaptation may also be unequal. A dollar spent reducing heat exposure for someone already close to the margin of serious harm may produce a very different benefit than the same dollar spent on someone whose exposure is already low.
