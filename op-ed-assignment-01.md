> Another will require as much electricity as an entire city.

---

## The Problem With Big Numbers

Large numbers immediately make us think that something must be wrong. But before deciding whether a data center is harmful, we should ask a simple question:

> **Compared to what?**

Water, electricity, and land are all resources with alternative uses. Using them for a data center means they cannot be used somewhere else. But that does not mean the data center is automatically creating a problem.

The real question is **what those resources are worth in their next-best use.**

For example, using large amounts of water is more concerning in a region already experiencing water shortages than in an area with abundant supplies. Similarly, a large increase in electricity demand may be manageable if the grid has available capacity, but much more costly if it requires new power plants or transmission infrastructure.

The same idea applies to land. Building a data center has different consequences depending on what that land could otherwise be used for.

---

## When Does Resource Use Become a Problem?

There are situations where a data center can create a genuine economic problem.

If it causes pollution, strains public infrastructure, or increases electricity costs for other users without paying for those consequences, then the data center is imposing costs on people who are not part of the transaction.

These are **negative externalities**.

Taxes, regulations, or other policies can help make those costs part of the decision.

But the solution is not necessarily to stop data-center development.

Restricting a project has costs too.

A community that prevents a data center may avoid some environmental burdens, but it may also lose:

* Jobs
* Investment
* Tax revenue
* Economic development

And if another community has fewer restrictions, the company may simply build there instead.

The demand for computing does not disappear.

**The economic and environmental effects are redistributed.**

---

## Look Beyond the First-Order Effect

This is where the conversation becomes more complicated.

If one community restricts data centers while another welcomes them, the original community may successfully avoid some local costs. But the project itself has not necessarily disappeared.

Instead, development, jobs, tax revenue, electricity demand, and environmental burdens may move somewhere else.

A policy that looks beneficial locally can therefore create different consequences elsewhere.

---

## The Number Is Only the Beginning

This is why headlines about how much water or electricity a data center will use are only the beginning of the conversation.

They tell us the **scale** of the project.

They do not tell us whether that scale is actually harmful.

To make that decision, we need to know:

1. How scarce are the resources?
2. What would those resources otherwise be used for?
3. Who bears the costs?
4. Who receives the benefits?
5. Is the data center paying for the costs it creates?

Without this information, a large number alone cannot determine whether a project is economically or environmentally harmful.

---

## So, What Should We Do?

Data centers may create real economic and environmental problems.

But a large number alone does not prove it.

The goal should not be to **build every data center** or **prevent every data center**.

The goal should be to create institutions that make the real costs and benefits visible, so communities can make informed decisions about what those resources are worth.

> **Don't just ask how big the number is.**
>
> **Ask what it means.**
