## Proposition 1: Turn Monroe County's Trash Into Electricity

### Should Monroe County establish a waste-to-energy pilot program to process up to 100,000 tons of non-recyclable municipal waste annually, generating electricity instead of sending that waste to landfills?

The county would solicit private operators, require modern air pollution controls and independent emissions monitoring, and dedicate 10% of annual electricity sales revenue to local environmental protection.

A YES vote authorizes the county to pursue the program, subject to permits and financing.

#### What's the deal?
Monroe County would divert up to 100,000 tons of non-recyclable municipal waste annually from landfills to a waste-to-energy facility. The waste would be burned under controlled conditions, producing steam to generate electricity.

According to the EPA, waste combustion can reduce landfill volume by approximately 85–95%, although residual ash still requires disposal. The environmental benefits could include reduced landfill demand, avoided methane generation, and additional electricity production.

In exchange, residents would accept local air emissions, additional truck traffic, and the financial cost of operating the facility. Private operators would finance construction through long-term contracts, waste-disposal fees, and electricity sales. The county would receive 10% of electricity sales revenue for environmental protection projects.

Residents benefit from reduced landfill dependence, while nearby communities bear the greatest environmental and health concerns.


#### BEST CASE for YES:
Landfills do not make waste disappear. They store it for decades, require land, and can release methane as organic materials decompose. Rather than continuing to bury waste, Monroe County could recover some of its energy value while reducing the amount of material requiring landfill space.

Waste-to-energy technology already exists and is commercially operated across the United States. Modern facilities use pollution control systems to limit particulate matter and other harmful emissions. Unlike solar and wind, these facilities can also generate electricity consistently when operating.

The proposal creates an additional incentive for environmental protection by directing 10% of electricity sales revenue toward local conservation and pollution mitigation.

If the facility replaces landfill disposal without reducing recycling, the county could gain useful energy while reducing its dependence on landfills.


#### BEST CASE for NO:
Burning trash does not eliminate pollution; it changes its form. Waste-to-energy facilities release carbon dioxide and can emit nitrogen oxides, particulates, and other pollutants. Even with modern filtration, emissions cannot be completely eliminated, and the remaining ash must still be disposed of safely.

Building a facility also requires substantial investment, potentially costing hundreds of millions of dollars. Long-term waste supply contracts may create financial incentives to keep burning trash rather than reducing waste generation or improving recycling.

Monroe County already operates a landfill that captures methane to generate electricity, meaning the additional environmental benefits may be smaller than expected.

The greatest concern is environmental justice. Nearby residents would experience the facility's emissions, noise, and truck traffic, while the electricity and broader environmental benefits would be distributed across the county. Is that a fair trade?

#### Biggest Uncertainty: 
The biggest uncertainty is whether incineration would actually reduce greenhouse gas emissions compared with Monroe County's existing landfill system, which already captures methane for electricity. Burning plastic releases fossil-derived carbon dioxide immediately, while landfill methane capture can reduce emissions from decomposing waste.

Another concern is that the facility would require a consistent waste supply to remain financially viable, potentially discouraging future recycling and waste reduction. The proposed 10% revenue contribution may also be too small to compensate communities exposed to additional pollution. The true environmental benefit depends on waste composition, emissions, costs, and displaced electricity generation.


#### Why isn't it already happening?
Monroe County already has an established landfill system, making waste-to-energy development economically difficult. Landfilling is often cheaper than constructing and operating an incinerator, especially when existing infrastructure is already paid for.

A new facility would require substantial financing, environmental permits, and long-term agreements guaranteeing enough waste to operate. These contracts create financial risks if waste generation decreases or recycling improves.

There is also a political problem: the benefits of electricity generation and reduced landfill demand would be shared across the county, but the costs of air pollution, truck traffic, and potential property value impacts would be concentrated near the facility.

Local residents may oppose construction even if the county benefits overall. Environmental organizations may also disagree over whether incineration counts as sustainable waste management. These competing incentives, combined with regulatory uncertainty and high initial costs, help explain why this technology has not replaced local landfills.
